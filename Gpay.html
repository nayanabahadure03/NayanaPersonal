Step 1

Make the intention visible

The user records what they already intend to protect. No full budget setup is required.

Step 2

Bring awareness forward

Instead of waiting until month-end, a weekly check-in surfaces recent Google Pay spending while there is still time to notice a change.

Step 3

Avoid false precision

The user decides whether they are On Track, Not Sure or Behind. The MVP does not claim to know every account, card or payment.

Step 4

Reward awareness, not restriction

Streaks reinforce the act of reviewing. A medical expense or family emergency should not feel like failure.

MVP boundary: Money Check-In does not prevent unexpected expenses, automatically protect savings, recalculate financial goals, or provide financial advice.

Speaker notes
Evidence & sources
Primary research — survey 68 total responses; 60 passed the behavioural screener. 57 qualified respondents were active savers/investors. Core figures in this deck use the qualified sample unless specified otherwise.
Primary research — interviews Four working professionals managing monthly income, expenses and savings/investment goals.
Key primary finding 33 of 57 active savers (57.9%) reported changing intended savings/investments after higher-than-expected spending.
Awareness finding 20 of 60 said they normally noticed overspending immediately; 35 of 60 recognised it later in the month, after low balance, or after a payment/balance signal.
Google Pay Help — transaction history Official Google Pay documentation states that Google Pay transaction history contains transactions made through Google Pay and does not represent all UPI or banking transactions.
Google Pay Help — Ask Google Pay Official documentation describes Ask Google Pay as a limited-rollout AI experience in India that can analyse spending patterns and provide money-management information. It does not initiate transactions.
Schomburgk, Belli & Hoffmann — Journal of Retailing, 2024 “Less cash, more splash? A meta-analysis on the cashless effect.” DOI: 10.1016/j.jretai.2024.05.003. Used only as broader behavioural context; it does not prove that Google Pay caused overspending in this sample.
Huebner, Fleisch & Ilic — Computers in Human Behavior, 2020 “Assisting mental accounting using smartphones: Increasing the salience of credit card transactions helps consumer reduce their spending.” DOI: 10.1016/j.chb.2020.106504. Used as supporting evidence that transaction salience and repeated feedback are relevant mechanisms.
